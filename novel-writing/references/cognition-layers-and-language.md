# Cognition Layers and Language

Use this file when character knowledge, judgment, motive, or dialogue strategy determines a consequential choice.

## Core Principle

Do not stop at whether a character knows something. Track how they classify it, what it lets them do, and what they choose to disclose.

Use two axes rather than forcing every cognition into one exclusive bucket.

## Axis One: Distribution and Source

- **Common or shared understanding**: assumptions the relevant community or participants normally share. Use it to constrain the scene; keep it invisible unless it is violated, disputed, or consequential.
- **Specialized or local understanding**: knowledge produced by training, investigation, institutional access, or sustained experience. Name its holder, source, confidence, and blind spots. It must change risk, options, or division of labor to deserve explanation.
- **Private or undisclosed understanding**: information not currently shared with the listener or group. Ask who holds it, how they acquired it, and whether they have reason to reveal it now.

These categories are relative to character, time, and audience. A medical fact may be shared among clinicians, specialized for a family member, and private until someone says it aloud.

## Axis Two: Epistemic Status

For each important cognition, distinguish:

- direct observation
- reported fact
- inference or judgment
- intention
- misrecognition, bias, or self-deception

A specialist judgment is not automatically a fact. A later-confirmed inference was still an inference when the character acted on it.

## Decision and Expression Chain

```text
available information and source
→ fact, inference, intention, or misrecognition
→ risk and options change
→ character chooses
→ character reveals, withholds, distorts, tests, or lies
→ listener responds
```

Use common understanding to set boundaries, specialized understanding to change available methods, and individual cognition to select and frame action. Treat this as a memory aid, not a rigid taxonomy.

## Dialogue Rules

- Do not make characters explain shared assumptions to each other for the reader's benefit. Show the assumption through procedure, action, object movement, or consequence.
- Reveal only the specialized detail needed to change the present decision. Professionals should not lecture merely to display research.
- Adjust vocabulary and precision to the listener: peer, outsider, superior, dependent, or adversary.
- Do not make a character fully confess a private intention when concealment, status, fear, or strategy argues against it.
- Show private cognition through selective wording, pauses, ordinary excuses, tests, lies, actions, and mistaken conclusions.
- When a reply is indirect, make the evasion, misunderstanding, or tactic legible. Do not mistake an accidental non sequitur for realistic dialogue.
- Do not let a speaker promise on behalf of absent people, institutions, or groups without authority.

## Common Failures

- **Common-knowledge manual**: characters explain the world to people who already live in it.
- **Professional lecture**: expertise adds terminology but changes no decision.
- **Author truth becomes consensus**: uncertain or hidden information appears in everyone's reasoning.
- **Villain confession**: an opponent states the full private strategy instead of pursuing it.
- **Static labels**: a cognition remains permanently classified despite a new audience, disclosure, or verification.
- **Knowledge without consequence**: the plan records what everyone knows but not what it changes.

## Review Questions

- Who holds this cognition, from what source, and at what confidence?
- Is it an observation, report, inference, intention, or mistake at this moment?
- What option, risk, or responsibility changes because of it?
- Why would this character say this much to this listener now?
- What remains unsaid, and how can the scene make that pressure visible?
