# Dialogue and Behavior

Use this file when planning, drafting, or reviewing a dialogue-heavy scene, especially a meeting, interview, consultation, interrogation, negotiation, domestic confrontation, or laboratory and research scene.

## Core Principle

Dialogue is behavior under pressure, not information arranged by speaker.

A scene does not become embodied merely because hands, cups, screens, files, or instruments move. The useful test is whether a character's action or reaction reveals an agenda, registers the impact of the previous beat, changes the next tactic, or alters the physical or social situation.

## Distinguish Three Kinds of Action

### Functional action

Functional action grows from the character's present motive and changes how the exchange proceeds. It may:

- expose concealment, hesitation, status, attraction, hostility, or expertise
- claim or surrender space
- interrupt access to an object or person
- make a lie harder to sustain
- force another character to revise the next question or decision
- create a physical or social consequence

The key test is: **the reaction changes the next beat**.

### Decorative action

Decorative action adds visible motion but carries no scene-specific meaning: smiling, frowning, nodding, propping up the chin, sipping a drink, or looking away when any character could do the same thing without affecting the exchange.

Decorative action is not forbidden. It becomes weak when used as a repeated speaker tag or as proof that a static scene is alive.

### Procedural action

Procedural action performs the task: opening a file, turning on a device, changing a sample, writing a note, moving equipment, or reading a result.

Procedural action may be necessary and technically accurate, but a scene full of procedural action can still be emotionally and socially static. Connect procedure to human pressure: who rushes, hesitates, protects a sample, hides a result, challenges an assumption, notices a mistake, or changes the test after a reaction.

## Build the Exchange as a Behavior Chain

For each consequential exchange, test this chain:

```text
speaker has a local objective
-> chooses what to say, omit, soften, or distort for this audience
-> listener receives it through a different objective and knowledge state
-> listener controls or fails to control a response
-> that response changes the next line, action, or tactic
```

Do not make every reply a complete answer. A plausible reply may evade, test, bargain, redirect, misunderstand, delay, or answer only the safest part.

Do not force a behavioral beat after every line. **Rapid dialogue may remain untagged** when the voices, spatial state, and pressure are already clear. Add beats at changes, not at a fixed density.

## Control Density and Rhythm

**Action density is not a quality target.** Do not distribute beats evenly across dialogue or give every sentence its own gesture, expression, tone label, and interior reaction.

Let routine information pass quickly. Let clean conflict run for several lines when interruption would weaken momentum. Slow down only where perception changes, pressure becomes bodily, a relationship shifts, or a choice acquires consequences. One precise reaction at the turn is usually stronger than several interchangeable movements around it.

## Where a Behavioral Beat Is Usually Needed

Look for a meaningful beat when:

- new information lands
- a private fact is nearly exposed
- a speaker lies, withholds, or changes their story
- status or control shifts
- someone decides whether to comply
- technical evidence contradicts a favored explanation
- restraint, confidence, or professional composure weakens
- a relationship becomes warmer, colder, safer, or more dangerous

The beat may be small. It must be specific to this person, pressure, and location.

## Do Not Replace Behavior with Emotion Labels

**Emotion labels are not a substitute** for dramatic behavior. "Angrily," "with a smile," "nervously," or "in surprise" may clarify delivery, but they do not show what the emotion makes the character do.

Likewise, a clenched fist, lowered gaze, or sip of water is not automatically meaningful. Ask:

- Why this response now?
- Is it voluntary, involuntary, or tactical?
- Who notices it?
- What does that observer do differently because of it?

If the answer is nothing, the action is probably decorative.

## Keep Viewpoint Selective

Do not cut to every participant for a reaction. Let the experiential center notice what this character would notice under present pressure, misread some of it, and miss the rest.

The viewpoint should organize:

- whose silence feels threatening
- which movement seems suspicious or reassuring
- what bodily discomfort competes with attention
- which object becomes charged with meaning
- what the viewpoint refuses to look at or think about

An objective sentence may briefly establish necessary movement, but repeated reaction shots disperse tension and make the scene feel staged.

## Dialogue-Heavy Professional Scenes

### Meetings

A meeting is not a queue of prepared speeches. Establish:

- who convened it and what decision is actually available
- who knows the evidence, who controls resources, and who can block action
- which statement changes the room rather than merely adding information
- which participants remain silent because speaking has a cost

Use interruption, document handling, selective questions, corrections, side-taking, delayed answers, and changes in authority only when they arise from those roles. Do not require every named participant to comment on every development.

### Interviews, consultations, and interrogations

Trust, identity, audience, and purpose must precede disclosure. A stranger should not receive a clean case summary merely because the reader needs one. Let the scene establish why the speaker talks, what they protect, and how the listener earns, infers, pressures, or fails to obtain information.

An improvised object or model may clarify thought, but it should arise from a demonstrated confusion and remain subordinate to the human exchange. Do not turn the other character into a passive student for an authorial lecture.

### Laboratory and research scenes

Separate instrument action from character action.

The apparatus may heat, scan, transmit, fail, or produce data. The characters still need distinct working behavior: one protects sample integrity, one pursues a bold hypothesis, one resists a risky test, one conceals relevant experience, or one notices that another person's reaction is itself evidence.

Technical explanation should answer a question created by evidence, failure, disagreement, or danger. If the same explanation could be delivered as a report after the scene with no loss, the present scene may be functioning as a laboratory log rather than fiction.

## Planning a Dialogue Scene

Before drafting a substantial exchange, identify:

- the scene-level decision or state change
- each consequential speaker's local objective
- what each person will not say directly
- the pressure carried by the viewpoint
- the usable space, objects, tasks, and interruptions
- the moment that forces a change of tactic

Do not preassign a speech to every participant. Plan responses and reversals, not a round-robin agenda.

## Revision Pass

1. Mark long runs where speakers alternate without the physical or social situation changing.
2. Classify nearby actions as functional action, decorative action, or procedural action.
3. Find the lines where information lands, a lie is tested, power shifts, or a decision changes.
4. Check whether the listener's response changes the next beat. If it does not, repair the interaction rather than decorating the sentence.
5. Remove interchangeable gestures and redundant emotion labels.
6. Preserve clean rapid dialogue where voice and pressure already carry the scene.
7. Recheck viewpoint: retain only the reactions this experiential center could notice and interpret now.

## Common Failed Repairs

- adding "angrily," "smiling," or "nervously" to every line
- rotating through nods, frowns, cups, chins, and glances as speaker tags
- giving every participant a reaction to prove the event matters
- inserting internal monologue after every spoken line
- replacing a technical lecture with the same lecture plus equipment motion
- turning a meeting into the same round-robin report with more facial expressions
- slowing all dialogue equally instead of slowing only consequential turns
- distributing action beats evenly instead of following the scene's changes in pressure

## Compact Example

Flat exchange:

```text
"The result is wrong," Mara said.
"It is not," Elliot said.
"What proves that?"
"The second sample."
```

Embodied exchange:

```text
Mara rotated the screen away before Elliot could touch it. "The result is wrong."

His hand stopped on the back of her chair. He looked at the sealed second sample, not the graph. "Then open that one."

She did not reach for it. The refusal answered him before she did.
```

The revision does not add motion for its own sake. Access to the sample becomes the argument, Mara's refusal exposes pressure, and Elliot's next move must change.
