# Story Outline and Causal Summary

Use this reference for an arc, volume, or whole-story outline, a story synopsis, or a standalone canon/theory document. These artifacts must let a first-time reader understand the story without access to prior chat, revision history, or the author's private notes.

## Core Principle

Write the causal story before explaining the system behind it.

A long-form outline is not an encyclopedia, a list of interesting events, or a defense of prior revisions. It is a compressed narrative that shows:

```text
pre-story state
-> story start
-> protagonist entry
-> investigation or pursuit
-> escalation and reversals
-> climax
-> resulting state
```

Only after that spine is complete should the document separate character arcs, system mechanics, author truth, and reveal boundaries.

## 1. Establish Only the Necessary Background

Before the inciting event, anchor the minimum pre-story state that makes the story possible:

- the place, institution, community, or system at the center of the story
- the concrete problem already present there
- why an informal organization, belief, market, or conflict arose in response
- the affected people whose situation will trigger the plot

Anchor a place, group, system, or important person before asking it to carry an event. A reader should not meet followers before the organization exists, a victim before their situation is known, or a special object before ownership and ordinary use are established.

Background is a necessary cause, not encyclopedic completeness. Explain only what the current story needs. Do not trace ownership history, institutional genealogy, or remote causes merely because the author knows them.

Mark the boundary between the pre-story state and the story start. Do not mix longstanding conditions with actions that occur after the protagonist enters.

## 2. Start the Story Through an Actor

State the first on-page change in concrete terms:

- who receives an opportunity, obligation, threat, or loss
- what immediate task they undertake
- who joins them and why
- what incident diverts or expands the original task

Do not replace this with a thematic sentence such as "the protagonist enters a hidden order." Show the assignment, team formation, encounter, or mistake that opens the plot.

## 3. Build Causal Bridges

For every major transition, make the following chain legible:

```text
new pressure or evidence
-> a specific actor interprets it
-> that actor pursues a goal
-> the actor chooses a method
-> the choice changes another person's options
-> a visible consequence creates the next event
```

At each turn, ask:

- Why this person?
- Why now?
- Why this method?
- What was already true before the decision?
- What did this decision newly cause?

Avoid summary jumps such as "attention increased, so the antagonist selected a victim" unless the outline explains what pressure the attention created, what the antagonist wanted, why that victim served the goal, and who executed the choice.

Events placed in chronological order are not automatically causal. If removing one event would not change the next decision, the bridge is missing or the event is not part of the main spine.

## 4. Control Information Order

Introduce information in the order a first-time reader needs it, not the order in which the author discovered or designed it.

Use four separate layers:

1. **Story facts:** the pre-story state and events that actually occur.
2. **Character knowledge:** what characters observe, infer, misunderstand, conceal, or decide at each stage.
3. **Author truth:** the hidden mechanism or relationship needed to keep the story internally consistent.
4. **Reveal boundary:** what the audience may reasonably confirm by the end of this arc or volume.

Do not let author truth become character knowledge. Do not place a later explanation into an earlier causal decision. Do not let a volume synopsis solve a mystery that the volume itself preserves.

Shared or common knowledge should normally constrain the outline without becoming an explanatory beat. Specialized knowledge belongs where it changes a method or decision. Private knowledge belongs with its holder and should remain concealed until disclosure has a motive and consequence.

## 5. Keep the Story Spine Dominant

The main outline should answer, in order:

1. What world and unresolved problem already exist?
2. Who enters, with what initial task?
3. What incident redirects that task?
4. How does the inquiry or pursuit widen?
5. Which parties respond, and what does each want?
6. Which decisions intensify the conflict?
7. Why does the climax happen to these people at this time?
8. What is changed, saved, lost, or still misunderstood at the end?

Mechanics, character profiles, themes, and reveal maps may follow the complete story. They must not interrupt the story every few paragraphs or become larger than the story they support.

## 6. Audit Objects, Identities, and Counts

Track consequential objects and roles explicitly:

- who owns or holds each object before and after a transfer
- whether two similar objects are actually distinct
- who knows about the transfer
- what capability the object has at that time
- whether a later use contradicts its established state

If an outline says one token was lost and another was later recovered, establish whether they belonged to different people. If the distinction is unnecessary, remove it rather than relying on the author's private memory.

Perform the same check for aliases, organizations, victims, teams, injuries, money, and elapsed time.

## 7. Write a Final-Form Document

A standalone outline or canon document states the current story positively. Omit drafting residue such as:

- "previously this was..."
- "this replaces..."
- "the author may decide..."
- responses to old criticism
- discarded alternatives
- explanations of why an earlier version was wrong

When a decision is genuinely unresolved, describe the functional requirement and the available slot without pretending it is settled. Do not make the whole document read like a revision log.

## Recommended Document Order

1. Scope and premise in one short paragraph
2. Pre-story state
3. Complete causal story spine
4. Character movement and opposing goals
5. System or world mechanics
6. Character knowledge versus author truth
7. Reveal boundary and end-state
8. Themes, only after the story demonstrates them

The premise summarizes the offer of the story. It does not replace the story spine.

## Failure Modes

- **Theory before story:** hidden mechanics appear before the reader knows the place, people, or incident.
- **Naked concepts:** an organization, believer, victim, or object acts before being anchored.
- **Background drift:** remote history grows while the immediate causal setup remains vague.
- **Chronology without causality:** events are listed in order but no decision produces the next event.
- **Author omniscience leak:** hidden truth is presented as if characters or readers have confirmed it.
- **Unmotivated selection:** an antagonist chooses a victim, ally, or method without a pressure and goal.
- **Object duplication:** a lost, transferred, or transformed object silently becomes two objects.
- **Mechanics dominance:** the technology, magic, or institution occupies more space than the human story.
- **Revision-log voice:** the document argues with earlier drafts instead of presenting the final story.

## Final Check

Before delivering, verify:

- Can a first-time reader retell the main story without reading the mechanics section?
- Is the pre-story state clearly separated from the story start?
- Is every major noun anchored before it performs narrative work?
- Does every major turn identify an actor, pressure, goal, choice, and consequence?
- Are character knowledge, author truth, and reveal boundary distinct?
- Are object ownership, identity, count, and transfer consistent?
- Is every background detail a necessary cause rather than encyclopedic completeness?
- Does the document state current canon instead of narrating the revision process?
